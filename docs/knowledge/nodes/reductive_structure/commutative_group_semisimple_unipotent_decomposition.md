# Commutative Algebraic Groups Split into Semisimple and Unipotent Parts

Let \(G\) be a commutative linear algebraic group over an algebraically
closed field. Let \(G_s\) be the set of semisimple elements of \(G\), and
let \(G_u\) be the set of unipotent elements of \(G\). Then \(G_s\) and
\(G_u\) are closed algebraic subgroups, and multiplication induces an
isomorphism of algebraic groups
\[
G_s \times G_u \xrightarrow{\sim} G.
\]

The subgroup \(G_s\) is diagonalizable, and \(G_u\) is unipotent.

*Proof.*  
For every \(g\in G\), the Jordan decomposition gives
\(g=g_sg_u=g_ug_s\). Since \(G\) is commutative, the semisimple and unipotent
parts of products are products of the corresponding parts:
\[
(gh)_s=g_sh_s,\qquad (gh)_u=g_uh_u.
\]
Thus \(G_s\) and \(G_u\) are subgroups and every element has a unique product
decomposition with factors in them.

Closedness follows by embedding \(G\) into a diagonal-plus-unipotent normal
form inside some \(\operatorname{GL}(V)\): semisimple and unipotent parts are
obtained by polynomial functions on the finite algebra generated by the
elements. The product map is bijective with inverse \(g\mapsto(g_s,g_u)\),
and these maps are morphisms. The semisimple commutative subgroup is
simultaneously diagonalizable, while \(G_u\) is unipotent by definition.
